**First check.** The working note tested one value of \(k\), while the draft allows any \(k\in[-1,1]\). That prompted me to check what the proposed budget requires of \(k\). In regime I, \(k+9/8\le 5/8\) requires \(k\le-1/2\). In regime II, \(3/4-k\le 5/8\) requires \(k\ge1/8\). These conditions are incompatible, so the draft’s guarantee cannot be printed.

**Replacement theorem.** The sharp budget for one \(k\) shared by both regimes is \(\boxed{15/16}\), attained uniquely at \(\boxed{k=-3/16}\). For every \(k\), the largest of the four coefficients is
\[
M(k)=\max\{3/4-k,\ k+9/8\},
\]
because these terms dominate \(-k\) and \(k+1/8\), respectively. The two terms sum to \(15/8\), so their maximum is at least \(15/16\). Equality requires them to be equal, which gives \(k=-3/16\). This admissible choice yields \((3/16,15/16)\) in regime I and \((15/16,-1/16)\) in regime II.

If the reader knows the regime before choosing \(k\), the original \(5/8\) budget can be retained. In regime I, \(k=-9/16\) makes both coefficients \(9/16\). In regime II, \(k=5/16\) makes both \(7/16\). Each value is optimal within its regime because it attains the average of that regime’s two coefficients, whose sum is fixed. A reader who must commit to one \(k\) in advance can use the common-\(k\) theorem only if the downstream argument tolerates \(15/16\).

The obstruction concerns the displayed upper exponents. Whether the observed error obeys a stronger bound by another argument remains open.

I verified the note’s four coefficients at \(k=-3/8\) and used that example only to motivate the feasibility check.
